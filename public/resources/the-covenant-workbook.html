Welcome to Your Premarital Journey

Congratulations on your engagement, and thank you for entrusting this season of preparation to a Christ-centered process. Premarital counseling is not a hurdle to clear before your wedding day — it is a gift you give your future marriage. Couples who invest time preparing their hearts, habits, and expectations before the wedding consistently report stronger, more resilient marriages afterward.

This workbook is designed to accompany six sessions with your counselor, pastor, or mentor couple. Each week builds on a core area of marital life that research and Scripture both identify as essential: your covenant foundation, communication, finances, parenting, family and self-care rhythms, and intimacy. You will read Scripture together (quoted from The Living Bible), work through exercises individually and as a couple, complete checklists, answer reflection questions, and fill out practical worksheets you can keep and revisit for years.

There is no way to “fail” this workbook. The goal is not perfection but honesty. Some conversations will be easy; others will stretch you. Bring your real selves — fears, hopes, histories, and all — into every page. God designed marriage to be a place where two imperfect people become one, safe, covenant-keeping team.

How to Use This Workbook

  • Set aside 60–90 minutes each week to complete the material together, ideally a few days before you meet with your counselor.
  • Answer the individual reflection questions honestly and privately before comparing answers — resist the urge to write what you think your partner wants to hear.
  • Use the checklists as conversation starters, not scorecards. Checking a box means “we talked about this,” not “we agree on everything.”
  • Keep a shared journal or notebook nearby for longer answers, and use the built-in “Download My Responses” button at the end of each week to save your work.
  • Bring unresolved questions or tender topics to your counseling session rather than trying to resolve everything alone.
  • Pray together before and after each session, even briefly. Invite God into the conversation, not just the calendar.
A note before you begin: This workbook is an educational and devotional resource for premarital preparation and is not a substitute for licensed mental health care, medical care, or crisis intervention. If you or your partner are experiencing domestic violence, abuse, addiction, untreated mental illness, or thoughts of self-harm, please tell your counselor immediately and contact a licensed professional or, in an emergency, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline) or 911.

Building on Christ

Your Covenant Foundation, Communication Basics & Love Languages

Covenant MarriageCommunication FoundationsLove Languages

Opening Devotional

Every home needs a foundation strong enough to bear the weight of a lifetime. Before you talk about budgets, in-laws, or bedtime routines, you need to agree on the ground you are building upon. The Bible describes marriage not as a contract between two consumers but as a covenant — a binding, sacred promise modeled on God's own faithfulness to His people.

A contract says, "I will do my part as long as you do yours." A covenant says, "I am yours, no matter what." Jesus Himself pointed back to marriage's original design when He was asked about it, quoting from Genesis to remind His listeners that God intended marriage to be a permanent, one-flesh union — not a business arrangement with an exit clause.

This week you will explore the difference between a contract mindset and a covenant mindset, begin building healthy communication habits, and discover how you and your partner each give and receive love. These three foundations — covenant commitment, clear communication, and love fluency — will support every other week of this workbook.

Best-Practice Teaching Points

  • Covenant marriage means your commitment is unconditional and permanent — not contingent on your partner's performance.
  • Healthy couples build an intentional “we” identity without erasing their individual identities, gifts, or friendships.
  • Most conflict in the first years of marriage is not about the topic on the surface (dishes, money, in-laws) — it's about whether each partner feels heard, valued, and safe.
  • Understanding how your partner gives and receives love (their “love language”) prevents years of well-intentioned effort that never quite lands.
  • A marriage built on Christ has a third partner in every decision — inviting God into your relationship from day one sets a pattern you'll return to for a lifetime.

Week 2 of 6

Speaking Truth in Love

Communication Patterns & Conflict Management

CommunicationConflict ManagementForgiveness

Opening Devotional

Every couple fights. The health of your marriage will not be measured by whether you disagree, but by how you disagree. James instructs believers to be “quick to listen, slow to speak, slow to become angry” — a rhythm that is nearly the opposite of our natural instinct in conflict, which is to speak first, listen last, and escalate fast.

Researchers who study marriage (most notably Dr. John Gottman) have found that the presence of conflict doesn't predict divorce — the presence of contempt, criticism, defensiveness, and stonewalling does. Scripture arrived at a similar conclusion two thousand years earlier: it is not disagreement itself but harsh words, unforgiveness, and pride that destroy a home.

This week you'll identify your own conflict style, learn a shared framework for fighting fair, and practice the muscle of forgiveness — because every marriage runs on forgiveness the way a car runs on fuel.

Best-Practice Teaching Points

  • Four communication habits predict marital breakdown: harsh criticism of character (not behavior), contempt or mockery, defensiveness, and stonewalling (shutting down/withdrawing).
  • “Fair fighting” means attacking the problem, not the person — use “I feel” statements instead of “you always/never” accusations.
  • A 20-minute cooling-off period, agreed upon in advance, prevents conflict from escalating past the point of productive conversation.
  • Forgiveness is a decision made in obedience to Christ, not a feeling that arrives on its own — it can be re-chosen daily even while healing continues.
  • Repair attempts (humor, an apology, a gentle touch, naming the escalation out loud) can de-escalate conflict before it spirals — identify what repair attempts work for your partner.

Week 3 of 6

Two Becoming One

Family Finances, Budgeting, Debt & Generosity

FinancesBudgetingDebtGenerosity

Opening Devotional

Money disagreements are one of the most common stressors cited by married couples, and financial infidelity — hidden debt, secret spending, undisclosed accounts — can wound a marriage as deeply as any other betrayal. Yet Scripture treats money not primarily as a math problem but as a discipleship issue: how you handle money reveals what you actually trust.

Proverbs repeatedly praises patient, disciplined stewardship over impulsive speculation, and warns that debt creates a kind of servitude. Paul teaches the Corinthian church that generosity should be cheerful and free, not coerced — a posture that must be negotiated between two people with possibly very different money histories.

This week you'll disclose your full financial picture to each other (yes, all of it), build your first shared budget, and talk honestly about giving, saving, and the money stories you each inherited from your families.

Best-Practice Teaching Points

  • Full financial disclosure before marriage — income, debt, credit score, spending habits, existing accounts — prevents devastating surprises later; hold nothing back.
  • Every person brings a “money story” from their family of origin (a scarcity mindset, a spender mindset, avoidance, control) that shapes their instincts even when unspoken.
  • Best practice is a written, agreed-upon budget reviewed monthly, with clear categories for giving, saving, debt repayment, and discretionary “no questions asked” spending money for each partner.
  • Couples benefit from deciding in advance who pays the bills, how accounts will be structured (joint, separate, or hybrid), and a dollar threshold above which both partners must agree before spending.
  • Generosity (tithing/giving) practiced from the start of a marriage builds a shared identity of trust in God's provision rather than scarcity and control.

Raising Up the Next Generation

Parenting Philosophy & Protecting Family Time

ParentingFamily TimeTraditions

Opening Devotional

Long before you hold your first child, your parenting philosophy is already being formed — by how you were raised, what you've observed in other families, and what you believe about discipline, grace, and faith formation. Couples who discuss parenting before marriage, rather than assuming they agree, avoid painful surprises when the first real disagreement about discipline or values arises.

Scripture places remarkable weight on intentional, everyday spiritual formation of children — not a once-a-week lesson, but a constant thread woven through ordinary moments: talking about faith at home, on walks, at bedtime, and first thing in the morning.

Equally important, and often neglected, is protecting family time itself. Family rhythms, traditions, and rituals are not extras; they are the glue that holds a household's identity together, whether or not children are part of your family's story yet.

Best-Practice Teaching Points

  • Discuss discipline philosophy before a child is born: how were you disciplined, what worked, what didn't, and what will you do differently or the same?
  • Agree on non-negotiables (safety, faith formation, education approach) and areas where you can stay flexible and learn together.
  • If children are not part of your plan, or if fertility, adoption, or blended-family dynamics are already part of your story, name that explicitly rather than assuming.
  • Family time is protected, not accidental — best-practice couples schedule recurring family rituals (meals, game nights, holidays, Sabbath rest) the same way they'd protect a work meeting.
  • Children (and marriages) thrive on predictable rhythms and traditions that create a felt sense of identity and belonging.

Honoring Every Relationship

In-Laws, Work/Life Balance & Healthcare Decisions

In-LawsWork/Life BalanceHealthcare Decisions

Opening Devotional

Marriage creates a new family unit — but it doesn't erase the families you came from. Scripture's picture of “leaving and cleaving” (Genesis 2:24) means your primary loyalty now belongs to your spouse, while you still honor your parents and welcome your new in-laws. Ruth's famous words to her mother-in-law Naomi show that in-law relationships, at their best, can be marked by deep loyalty and love rather than tension.

Alongside family boundaries, this week also addresses two often-overlooked areas of best-practice premarital preparation: work/life balance and healthcare decision-making. Jesus regularly withdrew from the crowds to rest, modeling a rhythm of work and rest that our current culture rarely honors. And healthcare — insurance, medical decision-making authority, and wellness habits — is a practical topic every couple should address before, not during, a health crisis.

Best-Practice Teaching Points

  • “Leaving” parents doesn't mean cutting them off — it means your spouse's needs and your shared decisions now take priority over any parent's preferences.
  • Agree in advance on boundaries: how much time with each set of parents, how holidays will be split, and how you'll present a united front when extended family pushes back.
  • Work/life balance is a couple decision, not just an individual one — discuss career ambitions, travel, hours, and how you'll protect time for each other and rest.
  • Best practice includes naming a healthcare power of attorney for each other, discussing insurance coverage, and agreeing on values around medical decision-making, mental health care, and preventive wellness.
  • A cheerful, healthy household requires proactively protected rest — physical, relational, and spiritual — not rest that only happens after burnout forces it.

Becoming One Flesh

Intimacy, Couple Time & Your Shared Marriage Vision

IntimacyCouples TimeMarriage Vision

Opening Devotional

God created physical intimacy as a good gift within marriage — a sign of covenant union, mutual belonging, and delight, not merely a physical act. Paul teaches that within marriage, husband and wife belong to one another's bodies, each seeking the other's fulfillment rather than only their own. Song of Solomon celebrates married love with unashamed poetic delight, reminding us that romance and desire are gifts from God, not topics to be embarrassed about.

Intimacy in marriage is far broader than the physical, though. Emotional intimacy (being fully known), spiritual intimacy (growing in faith together), and recreational intimacy (simply enjoying each other's company) all need deliberate cultivation — they rarely happen by accident once the busyness of life and, eventually, family sets in.

In this final week, you'll talk honestly about expectations, boundaries, and hopes around physical intimacy; build a plan to protect regular couple time for the rest of your marriage; and write a shared vision statement to carry with you from your wedding day forward.

Best-Practice Teaching Points

  • Physical intimacy in marriage is mutual: Scripture teaches that husband and wife belong to each other's bodies and are called to seek one another's fulfillment, not just their own.
  • Talk honestly before the wedding about expectations, past experiences, any trauma or purity-culture wounds that need healing, and how you'll approach the topic with grace, patience, and open communication.
  • Emotional and spiritual intimacy are built the same way physical intimacy is: through consistent, undistracted attention over time — not grand gestures alone.
  • Protect weekly couple time (a standing date night), an annual planning retreat, and small daily rituals (a hello/goodbye kiss, a nightly check-in) as non-negotiable marriage infrastructure.
  • A written marriage vision statement — a short shared paragraph naming your purpose, values, and hopes — gives you something to return to during hard seasons.
